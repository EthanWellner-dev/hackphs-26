Research summary

This page gathers the research that informs Aero-Hazard / Plume. It describes the incidents we are addressing, what controls airborne transport, how an estimated draft path is produced, how exposure limits should be read, and where a computer model must give way to field measurements and official direction. The page is intentionally text heavy so that a user can understand the assumptions behind the map before using it.

Incidents and response problems

Industrial releases, transportation accidents, fires, explosions, and radiological events can put hazardous material into the air before responders know the release rate, source height, chemical behavior, or local wind. The Bhopal gas disaster, the Chernobyl fallout event, and the East Palestine train derailment are different incidents, but they expose the same operational problem: decisions must be made while observations are incomplete and weather conditions are changing.

We are addressing the delay between an incident report and a usable first estimate. The simulator combines a reported source, a hazard library, terrain, buildings, and forecast weather so an incident commander can inspect possible downwind areas, compare time steps, and communicate uncertainty. It is a planning aid, not a replacement for monitoring, protective equipment, evacuation orders, or qualified incident command.

Useful initial questions are: What was released? Is the release continuous or instantaneous? Is the material a gas, vapor, aerosol, smoke, or radioactive material? Where is the source and how high is it? What is the wind at the source? Are people indoors, outdoors, uphill, downhill, or inside a ventilation path? Which observations would most quickly confirm or reject the first estimate?

Weather patterns and atmospheric stability

Wind direction determines the initial transport direction, while wind speed determines how quickly a plume moves away from its source. Gusts and directional shifts broaden the affected area. Temperature, humidity, cloud cover, and surface heating influence turbulence. A stable atmosphere suppresses vertical mixing and can keep a plume concentrated near the release level; an unstable atmosphere produces stronger vertical mixing and generally spreads material through a deeper layer. A neutral atmosphere falls between these behaviors.

Day and night cycles matter. Sun-warmed ground commonly creates daytime convection and stronger mixing, while nighttime surface cooling can create a stable layer close to the ground. In valleys and urban areas, local circulations can differ from the regional forecast. Sea breezes, drainage winds, fronts, thunderstorms, and terrain-driven flows can change direction or speed over a short distance.

Weather observations are snapshots. Forecast models interpolate between observations and may not resolve street-level effects. Terrain, trees, buildings, valleys, and walls can redirect or channel flow. For this reason, the map should be read as a range of plausible transport paths rather than a precise boundary.

How draft paths work with weather patterns

A draft path in this interface is an estimated advection path: the source location is moved through the local wind field over time. The calculation starts with wind direction and speed, then adds diffusion to represent unresolved turbulence. The result is visualized as a plume whose centerline follows the expected transport direction while its width represents mixing and uncertainty.

As the forecast timeline advances, the path can bend when the input weather changes. Terrain and building geometry can influence the displayed flow, but the simulation does not directly measure every corridor, roofline, or indoor ventilation system. A visible plume is therefore an analytical approximation of outdoor transport, not proof that a location is safe or contaminated.

The displayed path should be separated into three ideas. Advection is movement carried by the mean wind. Diffusion is the spreading caused by unresolved random motion and turbulence. Deposition or removal represents material leaving the air through settling, chemical reaction, rainout, or other processes. Different chemicals and particle sizes behave differently, so a gas model should not be interpreted as a particulate or fallout model.

Trees, buildings, and topography

Surface features change both the speed and direction of near-ground flow. Trees add drag and turbulence: a dense tree line can slow the mean wind, break a smooth plume into eddies, and increase mixing below and behind the canopy. Vegetation can also intercept some particles, but interception is not the same as removing a hazardous gas. A model that does not resolve individual trees should treat wooded areas as a source of additional uncertainty rather than as a guaranteed filter.

Buildings create wakes, recirculation zones, and channels. Wind accelerates around corners and through narrow street corridors, while the downwind side of a building can contain slower, rotating air. Roofs and taller structures can lift or divide a plume, and heating or exhaust systems can draw outdoor material indoors. Street-level wind can therefore differ substantially from the weather-station wind used by a regional forecast. Building footprints help describe geometry, but they do not replace measurements of entrances, HVAC intakes, alleys, or indoor pressure.

Topography redirects flow on larger scales. Ridges can force air upward, gaps can funnel it, and valleys can guide drainage winds or hold dense gas near low elevations under stable conditions. Slopes can produce upslope flow during daytime heating and downslope flow after nighttime cooling. Hills and terrain can also block a direct path while leaving higher or lower routes open. The displayed terrain-aware path is useful for identifying these possibilities, but its resolution and the available elevation data limit how much detail can be trusted.

These effects interact. A plume entering an urban street, forest edge, ravine, or hillside can change shape over tens of meters while the regional wind remains nearly unchanged. This is why the map should be used to select monitoring locations and response priorities, then updated with local observations. For background on atmospheric flow and dispersion modeling, see the National Weather Service mountain meteorology resource and the EPA dispersion-modeling resources.

Transport equations: from a wind path to a concentration field

Implementation boundary. The current simulator advances finite-mass Gaussian puffs through time-varying winds, sums their concentration fields with a ground-reflection term, and applies simplified spreading and removal rules. Terrain and building adjustments are approximations, not a resolved fluid-dynamics calculation. The equations below explain the model family and more advanced research directions; they do not claim that Aero-Hazard runs AERMOD, HYSPLIT, FLEXPART, RANS, or LES.

Unless stated otherwise, equations use SI units: concentration C in kg/m³, distance in m, time t in s, velocity in m/s, and mass in kg. Coordinates x, y, and z denote downwind, crosswind, and height above a flat reference surface. Mathematical operators ∇, ∇·, and ∇² mean gradient, divergence, and Laplacian. Each equation is a simplified teaching form with explicit assumptions, not a complete solver specification.

1. Advection–diffusion–reaction: the conservation equation

Here u is the three-dimensional mean velocity, K is an eddy-diffusivity tensor (m²/s), S is a volumetric source (kg/m³/s), and λ is a first-order loss rate (s⁻¹). Direction-dependent diffusivity allows horizontal and vertical mixing to differ. This form assumes a dilute tracer in approximately constant-density air; strongly varying density requires a density-weighted mass-fraction formulation. Initial concentrations, inflow/outflow conditions, and surface fluxes are as important as the equation itself.

Research implication: diffusion redistributes mass; it does not destroy it. In a closed, nonreactive domain with no surface loss, the volume integral of concentration must stay constant after emissions stop. A color disappearing from the map is not evidence that this physical mass has vanished. Particle and grid models are alternative ways to approximate this transport problem. [8]

2. Gaussian plumes and time-dependent puffs

Q is the release rate (kg/s), U is a constant along-wind speed, H is effective release height, and σy, σz are downwind-dependent spread lengths. The reflected image source at −H enforces zero flux through flat ground in this idealization. It does not represent deposition. Constant wind, a passive dilute tracer, and steady emissions make this a useful baseline, but not a street-canyon, dense-gas, or calm-wind solution.

The vector xp denotes a puff center; xp, yp, and Hp are its coordinate components. For constant homogeneous diffusivity, σi²(t) = σi,0² + 2Kit in each direction i. The simulator instead uses parameterized, stability-dependent spreading with a finite initial size and age-dependent broadening. Its simplified loss factors reduce retained puff mass rather than resolving surface exchange. A sequence of puffs can retain the history of a wind shift, unlike a single steady plume. At zero wind, the steady formula divides by zero; a finite-width puff can still spread.

EPA describes AERMOD as a steady-state plume model using boundary-layer turbulence and terrain treatments; it is more sophisticated than this elementary Gaussian equation. NOAA's HYSPLIT is a separate transport and dispersion system, not simply a line joining wind arrows. Neither system's published validation transfers automatically to this prototype. [6] [9]

3. Lagrangian stochastic particle transport

A numerical particle is a mass-carrying sample, not necessarily one physical aerosol. Thousands of trajectories can reconstruct a concentration distribution under evolving winds. The square-root time scaling is essential: doubling a time step must not double the random displacement amplitude. Spatially varying diffusivity requires a consistent drift correction and boundary treatment; blindly substituting K(X) in this equation can create artificial accumulation.

FLEXPART v10.4 uses stochastic velocity equations and well-mixed criteria rather than only this elementary displacement rule. Pisso et al. describe treatments for convective turbulence, density gradients, wet and dry deposition, gravitational settling, and chemical loss. This makes it a useful reference for a future particle engine, with increased meteorological-input and validation requirements. [8]

Complex flow: turbulence, stability, and density effects

4. RANS and LES for building-resolved flow

U is mean velocity, p̄ mean pressure, ρ0 reference air density, ν kinematic viscosity (m²/s), b body acceleration (m/s²), and R the velocity-covariance tensor. Primes denote velocity fluctuations and angle brackets an ensemble average. The extra covariance is unknown: a closure such as k–ε or k–ω estimates its effect. RANS can estimate mean building wakes at lower cost, but does not resolve individual gusts. [10]

Large-eddy simulation (LES) spatially filters the flow, resolves larger turbulent eddies, and models subgrid stresses. It can describe unsteady street-canyon recirculation more explicitly, but demands fine geometry, small time steps, suitable turbulent inflow, and substantial computing resources. Both approaches require a separate scalar-transport calculation to turn wind into contaminant concentration. Building footprints alone do not provide a validated CFD wind field. [11]

5. Surface-layer shear and atmospheric stability

u* is friction velocity, κ ≈ 0.4 is the von Kármán constant, d displacement height, z0 roughness length, and ψm a dimensionless stability correction. θ̄v is mean virtual potential temperature (K), g gravity, and ⟨w′θ′v⟩ the upward turbulent virtual-potential-temperature flux (K·m/s). Positive L indicates stable stratification; negative L indicates unstable stratification. In the neutral limit, the correction tends to zero and the logarithmic wind profile remains. This profile applies above the roughness elements, not inside individual building wakes or arbitrary forest canopies. EPA's AERMOD documentation provides the boundary-layer modeling context. [6]

6. Dense-gas gravity spreading and buoyant releases

ρc is cloud-mixture density, ρa ambient air density, h cloud depth, and g′ reduced gravity. Small Fr (large bulk Rib) indicates gravity spreading is important relative to the reference wind; this is a regime diagnostic, not a universal transition threshold or a trajectory prediction. Dilution and entrainment change both density and depth. Pure-substance molecular weight alone does not determine cloud density: temperature, composition, aerosols, and phase changes matter.

Large density contrasts require variable-density treatment beyond this approximation. Hot plumes instead gain positive buoyancy and may rise into different winds. A passive Gaussian model should not be assumed to reproduce either slumping dense-gas fronts or a pressurized jet near its source. NOAA's ALOHA technical documentation discusses separate Gaussian and heavy-gas approaches and their assumptions. [12]

Chemical fate, deposition, and time-integrated exposure

t½ is a process-specific half-life in seconds, Fd a downward mass flux (kg/m²/s), vd deposition velocity (m/s), and Cs concentration at the chosen near-surface reference height. Radioactive decay, chemical transformation, wet scavenging, and surface deposition are different processes. Daughter products or reaction products can remain hazardous. A single constant loss rate cannot represent arbitrary chemistry, reversible adsorption, or rain removal for every gas and particle size. [8]

The path x(t) follows a receptor and T is exposure duration. These expressions do not account for respirator performance, indoor penetration, uptake, or individual susceptibility. AEGLs are chemical- and duration-specific emergency guidelines with published durations of 10 minutes, 30 minutes, 1 hour, 4 hours, and 8 hours. A time average or a fixed concentration-times-time rule is not a universal way to translate between durations or severity tiers. Below AEGL-1 is not a guarantee of no effects. [13]

For an ideal gas at 25°C and 1 atmosphere, mg/m³ = ppm × molecular weight (g/mol) / 24.45. Other conditions require temperature/pressure correction. Do not apply gas ppm conversion to an aerosol mass field or interpret an airborne radioactive mass concentration as a radiation dose. A two-dimensional mass-per-area field also needs a justified vertical distribution before it becomes mass per volume. [14]

Uncertainty, numerical checks, and a research roadmap

Vary source strength, release timing, wind direction and speed, mixing, and removal assumptions together where they are physically related. An ensemble can reveal which monitoring priorities persist across plausible inputs. Its exceedance fraction is not a calibrated probability unless the members, weights, input distributions, and forecast reliability justify that interpretation. Instantaneous exceedance also does not establish exposure for an AEGL duration. This is a proposed analysis workflow, not an existing probability layer.

  • Conservation: check emitted mass against airborne inventory, deposited inventory, accounted transformations, and net boundary export. Keep renderer opacity and display culling separate from physical mass accounting.
  • Analytical benchmarks: test constant-wind translation, diffusion with variance growth 2Kt, reflected-puff normalization, and exponential loss before adding terrain or chemistry.
  • Convergence: reduce time steps and grid spacing or increase particle counts; compare concentrations and contours, not just animation smoothness. For a one-dimensional explicit upwind advection scheme, UΔt/Δx ≤ 1 is a stability condition; for explicit centered diffusion, KΔt/Δx² ≤ ½. Combined, multidimensional, and other schemes have different restrictions.
  • Observational validation: compare independent tracer measurements at matching locations, heights, units, and averaging periods. Report bias, error, arrival-time error, and missed exceedances; hold back measurements used for calibration. Include calm conditions, wind shifts, and near-source cases rather than validating only favorable weather.
  • Model selection: use simple analytical cases for baseline checks, time-dependent puffs or particles for changing winds, and validated building-resolved CFD only when geometry, boundary conditions, and resources support it. More equations do not compensate for an unknown source or inaccurate weather.

Next research steps: establish conservation and convergence benchmarks, quantify sensitivity to uncertain weather and emissions, validate against independent tracer data, and only then evaluate stochastic turbulence, improved deposition, or an offline CFD coupling. Report both accuracy and runtime so that increased physical detail is justified by measured improvement.

Source characterization and chemical behavior

The release rate, total mass, release duration, temperature, pressure, density, buoyancy, and source height all affect the result. A short, high-mass release creates a moving puff; a continuing leak creates a plume that is replenished while the leak continues. A warm buoyant release can rise before it is carried downwind. A dense gas can remain close to the surface or collect in low areas under suitable conditions. The same weather can produce very different paths for different materials.

When the source is uncertain, it is safer to compare multiple scenarios rather than select a single precise number. Test a low, central, and high release estimate; vary wind direction and speed; and inspect whether the priority area stays the same. Stable conclusions across those scenarios are more useful than a detailed-looking single run.

Safe levels and exposure language

Safety limits depend on the substance, exposure duration, route of exposure, and the population being protected. This project distinguishes trace, permissible exposure, and immediately dangerous to life or health thresholds where those values are available in the hazard library. These labels are screening references, not universal safe or unsafe guarantees. A short-term emergency guideline is not interchangeable with an occupational limit or a public-health standard.

EPA Acute Exposure Guideline Levels use different tiers to describe effects ranging from notable discomfort to serious or irreversible effects and life-threatening effects. NIOSH IDLH values are intended to identify conditions that could threaten life or prevent escape. Occupational exposure limits are generally designed around workplace assumptions and may not represent children, older adults, pregnant people, people with asthma, or the general public. Concentration alone also does not describe dose: time, breathing rate, protective equipment, and indoor filtration matter.

For an actual release, responders should use the current Safety Data Sheet, NIOSH Pocket Guide, EPA Acute Exposure Guideline Levels, applicable occupational limits, radiation-specific guidance when relevant, and instructions from emergency management. People should follow official alerts, shelter-in-place or evacuation orders, and responder directions. Do not enter a modeled plume to validate it.

Monitoring and decision support

A model is most useful when paired with observations. Wind meters, weather stations, fixed chemical sensors, personal monitors, air samples, visible indicators, and reports from trained responders can test the assumptions. Measurements should be logged with location, time, instrument, detection limit, calibration status, units, and whether the reading is a background, screening, or confirmation measurement.

Use the model to prioritize questions and resources: where to place monitors, which roads or buildings may be downwind, which facilities need an early notification, and how a wind shift could change the plan. Re-run when the source description, wind, stability, terrain assumption, or field readings change. Preserve old runs so that responders can see what changed and why.

Model limitations and responsible use

ALOHA and similar dispersion tools document important limitations: predictions become less reliable when the release description or weather inputs are uncertain, complex terrain and buildings are difficult to resolve, and indoor exposure requires a separate ventilation model. Aero-Hazard inherits these limitations. The output is intended to support questions such as where to collect measurements, which areas may need attention first, and how a changing wind could alter priorities.

The simulation does not calculate individual dose, guarantee evacuation boundaries, predict every indoor concentration, or certify that an uncolored area is safe. It also cannot know whether a chemical has reacted, pooled, ignited, or been removed by rain unless those processes are explicitly represented. Treat missing data as uncertainty rather than as evidence of no hazard.
